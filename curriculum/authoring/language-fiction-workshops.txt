# fiction-language
A precise noun selects a particular thing rather than adding a vague intensifier.|A rosette of daisies marked the lawn.|A very nice thing marked the place.|Rosette gives a shape and an identifiable plant; the vague version removes both.|A brass latch clicked against the gate.|A really good object made a sound.|The named material and mechanism make the action imaginable without an extra adjective list.|Explain how Mansfield's gardener and rosettes establish a deliberately prepared garden; analyse a second noun from the extract.
Active and passive constructions can distribute attention between a doer and an affected thing.|The window was sealed by the caretaker.|The window was closed and very sad.|Was sealed by contains a passive construction; a description is not automatically passive.|The letter was delivered before dawn.|The letter arrived before dawn.|The first is passive and can omit the deliverer; arrived is active even without a named human.|Compare the focus in the blue was veiled and a haze veiled the blue; avoid saying passive always means weakness.
Tense locates events; aspect shows whether an action is complete or continuing.|She had counted the coins before he arrived.|She counts the coins before he arrived.|The past perfect places the counting earlier than another past event.|He was waiting when the lights failed.|He waits when the lights failed.|The ongoing past action is interrupted by a completed event; the alternative shifts tense without a purpose.|Trace how O. Henry moves between saved pennies and the present problem; explain the order without rewriting the whole story.
Modal verbs can signal possibility, obligation or certainty, depending on context.|The visitor might return before dark.|The visitor must have returned because might means certainty.|Might leaves return possible, not established.|We must finish before the tide rises.|We might finish before the tide rises.|Must signals an obligation here whereas might expresses a possibility; neither gives a universal mood.|Analyse the narrator's uncertainty in Gilman's opening; compare one modal with a more categorical statement.
An adjective needs explaining through its noun and surrounding scene.|The shabby couch gives Della's distress a materially poor setting.|Shabby always makes a character dishonest.|The adjective describes the furnishing; character morality is not entailed.|The polished desk reflected the unused chair.|The desk was very extremely perfectly good.|Polished and unused can make an ordered space feel unoccupied; piling intensifiers adds little precision.|Analyse shabby and grey in the O. An adverbial can qualify time, manner or a speaker's confidence.|She almost smiled when the key turned.|She smiled, so almost makes no difference.|Almost limits the action and can complicate the apparent response.|For a moment, the platform was silent.|The platform was forever silent.|The time phrase bounds the silence and leaves room for a later interruption.|Explain the emphasis of literally hundreds in Mansfield; test whether it sounds like neutral counting or enthusiastic narration.
A simile creates a specific comparison, not a compulsory universal effect.|The bushes bowed as though visited by archangels.|The bushes are literally angels.|The comparison magnifies the garden's splendour without changing plants into beings.|Steam curled from the cup like a question mark.|Steam proves the speaker has asked a question.|The shape invites uncertainty as a possible association; the image is not literal dialogue.|Analyse Mansfield's archangels comparison alongside the ideal opening; qualify how the narrator may be heard.

Juxtaposition makes a relationship visible through placement or contrast.|Grey surroundings sit beside Della's affectionate Her Jim.|The grey setting proves affection is absent.|The contrast allows poverty and emotional value to coexist.|The polished reception desk faced a broken waiting chair.|The whole building must be equally luxurious.|Placing the two objects together makes unequal care noticeable without describing every room.|Explain how O. Dialogue can imply a relationship through what is said and how it is framed.|Treat me as an honoured guest can sound like withdrawal from responsibility.|The mother gives a direct list of practical arrangements.|The invitation to treat her as a guest changes her role rather than supplying the requested location.|Of course, take your time, he said, looking at his watch.|Of course always guarantees genuine patience.|The glance may complicate the literal reassurance; tone must be supported by the surrounding action.|Analyse the mother's reply and Laura's response in Mansfield, distinguishing a possible implication from a confirmed intention.
Narrative voice is a constructed speaking position, not transparent biography.|Gilman's first-person narrator lets us hear disagreement that she conceals socially.|Every I in fiction records the author's literal experience.|Private reflection belongs to this narrator unless external evidence establishes autobiography.|The narrator calls the room splendid while listing its defects.|The author necessarily approves every adjective spoken by the narrator.|The contradiction can invite a reading of irony or unreliable judgment without proving a single intention.|Compare the access offered by Gilman's I with O. Actions can suggest character without supplying an entire private history.|Laura blushes and tries to appear severe.|Laura is certainly a cruel employer for the rest of her life.|The small adjustment suggests self-conscious performance, not a complete moral biography.|He smoothed the unused ticket before putting it away.|He definitely hated every journey he had taken.|Repeated care may imply value or hesitation, but the cause remains open.|Analyse Laura's bread-and-butter and attempt to look severe as two connected actions.

Alternative readings need unequal weighing against actual evidence.|John's practicality can sound reassuring yet also dismissive of the narrator.|Two interpretations exist, therefore both are automatically equally convincing.|Competing views remain accountable to detail, tone and context.|Laura's self-consciousness may suggest concern about manners rather than certainty about authority.|Any plausible motive is an established fact.|Her observable behaviour supports a limited reading; certainty about private causes goes further.|Test two readings of John or Laura and explain which details strengthen or limit each.

# fiction-structure
Orientation establishes what a reader needs while leaving purposeful questions.|A place, an action and a viewpoint are established before the unexplained knock.|Every character's life story is explained before anything happens.|Enough orientation permits interpretation; total background can delay the central situation.|The room is identified before its locked door becomes significant.|A door is mentioned without any location or relation to the viewpoint.|The first supplies a usable spatial relation while preserving curiosity.|Trace the opening orientation of the authentic extract and rewrite a short opening that controls what is known.
An opening question can arise through a gap in events rather than a literal question mark.|Della counts a precise sum before the gift problem is explained.|The text opens with a complete account of every later event.|The small amount invites inquiry which subsequent context develops.|A returned parcel waits on a table before its sender is named.|The narrator immediately explains the sender and every consequence.|The first creates an information gap without removing all orientation.|Explain the first question created in the extract and how the next paragraph begins to answer or redirect it.
Focus can narrow from a whole scene to a small revealing detail.|The prepared garden gives way to Laura holding bread-and-butter.|The viewpoint remains on the whole garden without any change.|A smaller action brings attention to Laura's social discomfort.|The harbour is established before attention settles on one fraying knot.|The scene changes continent without a transition.|The change of scale is a focused narrowing, not a random location jump.|Map three successive scales of attention in the authentic extract and explain one effect.
A change of place alters what can be observed or understood.|The narrative moves from the flat to the vestibule and back to Della.|Every place is described as though it were the same room.|A spatial shift can enlarge social context before returning to a person.|Moving from a bright shop into a narrow yard changes available light and sound.|The writer adds a new place name that has no relation to the scene.|The transition changes the reader's sensory access and can reshape expectation.|Explain why the writer changes location at a chosen point; identify what becomes newly visible.
A time shift needs a clear anchor and a reason for leaving the present.|Earlier prosperity is recalled to explain the faded name-card.|The narration changes tense without any temporal relation.|The shift supplies a past contrast that alters the current household's significance.|Years earlier, the gate had opened onto fields; now it faced flats.|The gate changes its history without any time marker.|The marker makes a contrast across time legible.|Trace one past reference in O. Chronology can make causes and consequences clear without becoming a plot list.|Counting money leads to distress, observation and a new decision.|A summary records objects but omits how one event leads to another.|The causal sequence explains development rather than merely order.|She finds the broken seal, checks the address and returns the parcel.|The parcel is described in three unrelated colours.|The ordered actions form a response to a discovered problem.|Produce a causal sequence of the extract, then explain why one event is placed before another.
A flashback should change the reader's understanding of the current moment.|The name-card recalls a time of higher income while the flat remains shabby.|A memory is included only because every story needs one.|The recalled prosperity sharpens the present financial contrast.|A familiar smell prompts a brief memory before the traveller chooses to enter.|A childhood biography interrupts the scene without returning to it.|The memory changes the decision and is integrated with the present action.|Explain how a recollection affects the present scene; do not simply label past tense a flashback.
Foreshadowing invites a later possibility without guaranteeing it.|The damaged catch is noticed before the storm intensifies.|Every broken object proves a death will occur.|A planted detail can shape anticipation while its later use remains open.|A character tests a weak torch before entering an unlit passage.|The torch guarantees exactly what happens inside.|The weakness prepares uncertainty rather than revealing the outcome.|Distinguish an actual planted detail from hindsight in the authentic extract; explain the limit of the prediction.

A transition establishes a relation between paragraphs, not just a linking word.|The room description follows the tears and extends their material setting.|Every new paragraph must begin Furthermore.|The relation can be spatial or causal without a formulaic connective.|A paragraph ends at a locked gate; the next starts on its other side.|The next paragraph lists an unrelated holiday destination.|The boundary marks a purposeful change in access or action.|Explain a paragraph boundary in the extract; write two alternative transitions and justify one.
Narrative pace is the relation between text space and event time.|Several sentences linger on Della's repeated counting.|Every short sentence must describe a fast event.|A brief action can receive extended attention; length alone does not fix pace.|One sentence passes over a year before a paragraph lingers on a handshake.|One sentence always takes the same time to happen as another.|The changing amount of textual attention differentiates summary and scene.|Identify compression and expansion in the assigned text; explain why one moment receives more attention.

A return to an earlier image can give it a changed meaning.|The counted money returns as the limit on an affectionate plan.|Repetition means the ending cannot develop anything.|The returned detail carries additional stakes after intervening information.|The opening unanswered bell returns after the visitor has left.|The second bell must have the same effect as the first.|Changed context can make the same sound signify a lost possibility.|Trace a return or repeated detail in the authentic extract and explain what the intervening material changes.

# evaluation
