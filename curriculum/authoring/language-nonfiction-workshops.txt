# nonfiction-reading
Purpose and audience are inferred from choices and context rather than guessed from a label.|A memoir recalls experience for later readers and shapes how the writer is understood.|Every first-person text is a private diary written at the moment.|Publication and retrospective selection distinguish a memoir from an immediate record.|A campaign letter asks an identified decision-maker for a change.|Any text containing facts has no persuasive purpose.|Information can serve a persuasive aim.|Compare the purposes of the two authentic accounts and support each inference with content and presentation.
Explicit information is what the source actually states within the selected section.|Seacole describes learning by observing her mother and her patients.|Seacole's exact later success rate can be calculated from the childhood account.|The passage gives an apprenticeship narrative, not statistical outcome data.|Muir says money was scarce before the mountain opportunity.|The passage states that money never affected his choices.|The financial concern is directly reported.|Retrieve two accurate details from each source and keep observation separate from inference.
Inference must connect detail with a proportionate interpretation.|Seacole's childhood play can suggest a longstanding interest in care.|A childhood doll proves every later medical decision was correct.|The detail supports interest, not a guarantee about practice.|Muir's wish to study scenery helps explain his willingness to consider the work.|The wish proves no other motive could exist.|A plausible motive is not necessarily exclusive.|Infer each writer's attitude to the work or opportunity, then explain the limits of the inference.
A statement can be true elsewhere but unsupported in the specified section.|I check whether the selected paragraph actually supplies the claimed detail.|I accept a statement because it sounds historically likely.|Task scope limits the admissible evidence.|A statement about the narrator's birthplace is checked against the given excerpt, not remembered biography.|Any fact about the author automatically answers every source question.|Relevant source evidence matters more than outside knowledge.|Mark a source section and test four claims against only that section; explain any unsupported one.
Historical sources require contextual vocabulary without treating their assumptions as present-day facts.|Doctress is explained as a historical term used for a woman practising healing.|The old vocabulary is copied as proof that all medical ideas then were correct.|Vocabulary support enables understanding while preserving critical distance.|A source's date of publication is distinguished from the period it recalls.|A memoir published later must describe only its publication year.|Writing and remembered experience can belong to different dates.|Explain two unfamiliar historical terms and distinguish the source's publication date from the events recalled.
Literary non-fiction combines factual claims with a crafted voice.|Seacole's address to readers shapes a sociable, self-presenting memoir voice.|A memoir's entertaining tone makes all its events fictional.|Crafted presentation does not by itself cancel factual intent.|Muir's enthusiastic description presents a particular relation with the mountains.|Vivid language guarantees an entirely neutral report.|A factual account can also have a selective, expressive perspective.|Analyse a narrative or descriptive choice in each authentic account without calling either a neutral transcript.
A factual claim can be checked; an opinion evaluates or interprets.|The account names a location, then describes it as wonderful.|Wonderful is a measured scientific quantity.|An evaluative adjective expresses a judgment rather than a simple location fact.|A report gives a time, then calls the delay unacceptable.|Unacceptable must be an independently verified fact because it is printed.|Facts and evaluations may appear in the same sentence.|Separate a stated detail, an evaluative judgment and an inference in each source.
Bias can appear through selection, emphasis or unsupported generalisation.|A writer foregrounds personal success while giving little space to competing explanations.|A personal viewpoint makes every factual statement automatically false.|Selection calls for scrutiny, not blanket dismissal.|An account uses one encounter to generalise about an entire community.|One example necessarily represents every member of a group.|The scope of a generalisation may exceed its evidence.|Identify a source's selective emphasis; distinguish a supported interpretation from an unsupported general claim.
A summary prioritises relevant ideas rather than reproducing every detail.|The summary condenses how Seacole developed an interest in practice.|The summary quotes every sentence in its original order.|A summary selects the relevant thread and compresses it accurately.|The summary traces Muir's wish, financial obstacle and offered opportunity.|The summary lists every adjective about scenery.|The selected causal thread answers a purpose more effectively than an inventory.|Summarise each writer's preparation or motivation in three sentences without adding analysis of devices.
A similarity must be inferred from evidence in both sources.|Both accounts connect an early interest with a later opportunity to act.|Both writers had identical experiences because each uses I.|A shared pattern can coexist with different situations.|Both writers describe a practical barrier alongside a desired activity.|One source's detail is repeated as though it appears in both.|A similarity needs support from each text.|Write two inferred similarities, pairing a different reference from each source for each claim.
A difference should relate the same aspect across both sources.|One account develops childhood learning while the other foregrounds negotiating an opportunity.|The writers differ because one mentions a doll and the other mentions sheep, with no explanation.|The comparison interprets what the details show about the selected aspect.|One writer presents knowledge as inherited through observation, another as pursued through work and travel.|One author has more letters in their name.|The difference concerns the sources' presented experiences.|Compare the writers' preparation or motivation and explain the inference supported by each detail.
Paired evidence should address the same comparative claim.|I choose a detail about learning from each source.|I compare a childhood action with an unrelated publication date and give no link.|Comparable evidence lets the reader test a shared aspect.|A claim about practical difficulty uses a relevant obstacle from each text.|A strong quotation from one source replaces any evidence from the other.|Both sides are needed for a paired conclusion.|Select and justify an evidence pair for one similarity and one difference.
Synthesis combines inferred information; methods analysis answers a different demand.|Both accounts suggest that interest motivates action, supported by one detail each.|The answer only names a simile in each source.|The first combines information; the second shifts to methods before answering the inference task.|A summary explains a difference in motivation without discussing alliteration.|The summary becomes a list of rhetorical devices.|The task's demand should determine what is included.|Write a concise synthesis about motivation, then write a separate methods comparison and explain the difference between the tasks.
Travel writing positions the observer in relation to unfamiliar places and people.|Muir's longing shapes his account of the mountain opportunity.|His enthusiasm proves every traveller would respond identically.|The viewpoint belongs to a situated observer.|A travel account selects discomfort as well as scenic interest.|Any positive adjective makes the account entirely promotional.|A mixed selection may qualify the overall attitude.|Analyse a travel writer's selected details and consider what a differently placed observer might notice.
A retrospective account can select and interpret an earlier experience.|A later narrator describes childhood play as a beginning of later practice.|The childhood self necessarily understood every later consequence.|Hindsight can organise events into a developing life story.|A published recollection frames a past decision through later understanding.|A printed date proves the prose was written during the event.|Event time, writing time and publication need distinguishing.|Explain how retrospective knowledge may shape either authentic account; avoid claiming access to the author's private motives.
Journalistic framing makes an issue salient through selection, order and attribution.|An original model article leads with a missed service and then identifies its evidence.|The model invents eyewitnesses and calls them verified.|A compelling lead still needs honest provenance.|An article separates the reporter's account from an attributed opinion.|Every opinion in an article is presented as the journalist's own fact.|Attribution helps readers evaluate a claim's source.|Write two differently framed leads using only the same supplied facts, then explain what each foregrounds.
An argument's emphasis should be tested against what its evidence can establish.|A writer emphasises a recurring obstacle and then proposes a bounded remedy.|A writer treats one anecdote as proof of an inevitable national outcome.|Emphasis can make a problem visible but cannot enlarge the evidence automatically.|An objection is acknowledged before its practical significance is weighed.|Opposing information is hidden so the argument appears certain.|A credible case makes its limits visible.|Identify an account's emphasis and write a reasoned challenge to a claim that goes beyond its evidence.
Non-fiction language analysis explains how wording conveys a perspective.|Muir's longing and joyful independence connect travel with desired freedom.|The answer says the text has adjectives and stops.|A relevant word pattern contributes to an identifiable attitude.|Seacole's playful doll anecdote makes an early interest engaging to later readers.|The anecdote proves every reader laughs.|The method shapes a possible response, not a universal reaction.|Analyse two connected language choices in one source, then compare a contrasting choice in the other.
Tone and register emerge from a pattern of choices rather than a single label.|Direct reader address and playful qualification create a conversational memoir voice.|Any first-person pronoun makes every text informal.|Pronouns need considering alongside syntax, vocabulary and purpose.|A formal report maintains precision while an account foregrounds personal response.|Long words automatically make writing more reliable.|Register and reliability are related questions, not identical measures.|Describe each source's tone with evidence, then qualify a label that does not fit the whole excerpt.
Comparing ideas means relating what writers present about a shared issue.|Both writers value purposeful activity, but their routes into it differ.|The answer gives two separate summaries and never connects them.|The shared issue provides a basis for a developed comparison.|A paragraph moves between two attitudes to practical obstacles.|One writer is discussed at length and the other appears only in the conclusion.|Balanced relation matters more than equal quotation length.|Compare the accounts' ideas about learning, work or opportunity in a connected paragraph.
Conveyed perspectives require both the attitude and the means used to present it.|I compare the writers' attitudes and explain how anecdote and description shape them.|I list similar words without identifying either viewpoint.|Methods become relevant through their contribution to perspective.|One writer's retrospective humour is related to another's idealising description.|The answer merely says both use language.|The comparison needs a specific relation between methods and attitudes.|Write a comparison linking a shared issue, a difference of perspective and a meaningful method in each source.
A linked paragraph should make the relationship visible throughout.|A comparative claim is tested through one source and then related to the other.|An unrelated paragraph about each writer is joined by whereas.|A connective cannot supply a missing comparative basis.|A final sentence explains why the difference matters for the shared issue.|The paragraph ends by repeating both quotations verbatim.|Interpretation should develop the relationship rather than restate its evidence.|Write a comparative paragraph and underline every sentence that genuinely relates the sources; revise an isolated one.
Independent paired reading requires fresh evidence selection and clear task separation.|The response distinguishes synthesis from comparison of perspectives.|Every task receives the same prepared device paragraph.|Different demands need different evidence and reasoning.|A paired response explains both an inferred difference and how each voice conveys attitude.|Only one source is read because the topics look similar.|The comparison cannot be supported by assumptions about the unread text.|Complete the independent paired-reading task before viewing criteria; label any previously studied source as familiar practice.
A comparison conference should test the relation and the evidence on both sides.|A reviewer identifies where the second source does not support the stated difference.|The reviewer asks for equal quotation length as the only improvement.|The weakness concerns relevance, not mechanical balance.|A reader names the shared issue and checks whether each paragraph returns to it.|The reader adds a grade without identifying any passage.|Useful feedback tests the actual argument.|Ask a reader to identify one weak evidence pair, record the feedback and replace or reinterpret that pair.
Delayed comparison transfer tests a new shared issue without copying the old conclusion.|I compare a different aspect and select evidence anew.|I substitute author names into an old claim that does not fit.|Transfer requires a fresh relation between the texts.|I explain which earlier comparison skill helped without pretending the answer is unseen if familiar.|I memorise a verdict and apply it regardless of the sources.|Honest conditions and new reasoning matter.|Complete a new paired task, then explain one change in your comparative reasoning and one remaining uncertainty.
# accuracy
A sentence boundary separates independent clauses unless a suitable joining construction is used.|The door opened. Nobody entered.|The door opened, nobody entered.|Two independent clauses need a full stop, semicolon or suitable conjunction here.|The train stopped; the doors stayed shut.|The train stopped the doors stayed shut.|The semicolon joins related complete clauses without a run-on.|Edit a paragraph for boundaries and explain two decisions; do not split a clause away from the words it depends on.
A subordinate clause needs a complete main clause in this formal sentence.|Although the room was empty, the lamp was warm.|Although the room was empty.|The first gives the dependent clause a completed main statement.|Because the bus was late, we walked.|Because the bus was late and then.|The second leaves its grammatical relation unfinished.|Rewrite a paragraph using a controlled subordinate clause and explain what information it foregrounds.
A comma can mark a clear introductory boundary but cannot fix every sentence problem.|After the bell rang, Mina closed her book.|After, the bell rang Mina closed her book.|The first marks the boundary after the complete introductory clause.|When the rain stopped, the crowd moved.|When, the rain stopped the crowd moved.|The comma belongs at the clause boundary, not simply after the first word.|Edit commas in a short passage; distinguish a useful boundary from a comma splice.
Apostrophes can mark possession or omitted letters, not ordinary plurals.|The student's coat hung beside the door.|The students coat's hung beside the door.|A singular student's possession differs from plural coats.|The children's bags were ready.|The childrens' bags were ready.|Children is already plural, so its possessive takes apostrophe-s.|Write and explain singular possession, plural possession and a contraction; check that no ordinary plural receives an apostrophe.
Punctuation should clarify relations among ideas.|She needed three things: a map, a torch and a key.|She needed: because the journey was long.|The first colon introduces an explanation of a complete lead-in.|The door was locked; the window remained open.|Although the door; was locked.|The semicolon joins complete related clauses, not part of a dependent clause.|Revise a paragraph with purposeful punctuation and explain why a simpler alternative might also work.
Sentence variety should reflect meaning rather than a quota.|A short command interrupts a longer observation.|Every sentence is shortened merely to create a pattern.|The contrast has a relation to the moment's action.|A longer sentence gathers linked observations before a brief decision.|A writer inserts complex sentences whether or not their clauses connect.|Control matters more than counting sentence types.|Write a paragraph with two deliberately different sentence forms and explain their local purpose.
Agreement and tense consistency make relations intelligible.|The group was waiting beside the gate.|The group were waiting beside the gate is always wrong in every context.|British English can use collective agreement by sense; judge consistency and intended meaning.|The two visitors were waiting when the guard arrived.|The two visitors was waiting when the guard arrives.|Plural agreement and a controlled past sequence suit this version.|Edit a paragraph for subject-verb agreement, pronoun reference and purposeful tense; recognise legitimate British collective agreement.
Spelling should be checked through meaning, patterns and reliable reference.|Their coats were left over there.|There coats were left over their.|The homophones have different grammatical functions.|The weather affected the journey.|The weather effected the journey is the same meaning.|Affected means influenced here; effected normally means brought about.|Edit a passage for commonly confused words; explain why a plausible spelling checker suggestion may still be wrong.
Precision editing should retain meaning while improving audience fit.|Please confirm whether the room will be available after four.|Tell me the room thing now, written as a formal request.|The first identifies the information and uses an appropriate relation with the recipient.|The proposal could reduce one access barrier.|The proposal will certainly solve every educational problem.|The narrower wording is clearer about the claim's scope.|Edit your own viewpoint paragraph for precision and register without replacing every simple word with a longer one.
Independent editing needs explaining as well as correcting choices.|I mark the original error, revise it and explain the grammatical or rhetorical reason.|I accept every automatic correction without rereading its meaning.|An edit should improve the intended sentence, not merely satisfy a tool.|I compare a boundary change with the original paragraph's rhythm and clarity.|I change punctuation randomly until the text looks sophisticated.|Control includes retaining an effective simple construction.|Complete the independent editing passage, then apply the same checks to an earlier saved draft.
# spoken
A presentation topic must be manageable and agreed through the school's assessment process.|I propose one clear question and agree the scope with my teacher.|I assume a completed app quiz awards the speaking endorsement.|The actual presentation and school assessment remain essential.|A focused local proposal can be developed within a short formal talk.|A topic covering every global issue needs no narrowing.|A bounded question permits organised depth.|Propose a topic, identify the audience and seek actual teacher agreement; record the agreed scope without inventing approval.
Research supports a talk when sources are reliable and claims remain within scope.|I record the author, date and source of a claim and check it against another source.|I use an impressive number from an unattributed image as proof.|Provenance and verification matter more than apparent authority.|A clearly labelled personal example illustrates a point without pretending to be a representative survey.|One anecdote proves every person's experience.|An example's status limits its evidential reach.|Prepare a source log and check two intended claims; distinguish evidence from opinion and uncertainty.
A formal audience needs a clear purpose and accessible explanation.|I explain a necessary term before relying on it.|I assume every listener has read my notes.|Listeners cannot easily reconstruct missing background while the talk continues.|I adapt examples to a mixed audience without assuming shared experience.|I tell every listener what they must personally believe.|Respectful orientation supports engagement.|Prepare an introduction that identifies the question, purpose and route for your actual audience.
Clear spoken organisation helps listeners follow a developing argument.|I signpost a transition from the problem to two possible responses.|I change topic without explaining the relation.|Audible structure supports comprehension without visible headings.|A concluding return explains how the evidence answers the opening question.|The conclusion introduces an entirely new topic.|The ending should resolve the talk's developed purpose.|Outline and rehearse three sections with clear transitions; ask a listener to retell the route.
Audience engagement can arise from a relevant example or question without demanding agreement.|I ask a focused question and allow time for listeners to consider it.|I use repeated insults to force attention.|Engagement should serve understanding, not intimidation.|A brief concrete example makes the proposal's stakes imaginable.|A dramatic unrelated anecdote replaces the talk's actual evidence.|Relevance matters as much as vividness.|Try two introductions aloud with a listener; record which gives them a clearer reason to listen and why.
Audibility means being intelligible to the intended audience, not shouting continuously.|I test pace and volume in the actual room and respond to listener feedback.|I assume a louder voice always produces a better presentation.|Delivery needs adapting to room, content and listeners.|A pause separates an important contrast and allows processing time.|Every pause proves that a speaker lacks knowledge.|A purposeful pause can support clarity.|Rehearse aloud to a listener, check audibility and pace, then repeat a section after actual feedback.
Spoken Standard English should be appropriate to the formal setting without treating accent as an error.|I keep my accent while choosing clear grammar and appropriate vocabulary.|I must imitate one particular accent to demonstrate understanding.|Accent and appropriate Standard English are not the same issue.|I explain technical language and avoid an unclear pronoun reference.|Every regional pronunciation needs correction.|The priority is effective, intelligible formal communication.|Rehearse a paragraph in your own voice; revise unclear wording while retaining a natural, appropriate delivery.
Listening includes checking what a question asks before answering.|I briefly clarify whether the listener is asking about cost or access.|I answer a memorised question regardless of what was asked.|Clarification helps produce a relevant response.|I paraphrase a complex question before responding and check that I understood.|I speak over the question so that the talk never pauses.|Listening is an active part of the assessed interaction.|Ask a listener for two real questions; record your understanding, clarification if needed and actual responses.
A strong response addresses the question, supports it and acknowledges limits.|I explain why the evidence supports a trial but not a guaranteed national outcome.|I invent a fact to avoid admitting uncertainty.|Honest limits support a credible exchange.|I accept a valid correction and explain how it changes the proposal.|I repeat the original claim more loudly to avoid the feedback.|Responsive reasoning can refine rather than abandon the central position.|Rehearse follow-up questions with a listener; record one answer improved after feedback and one point to research.
