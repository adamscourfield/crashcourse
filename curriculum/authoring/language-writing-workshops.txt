# creative-writing
Purpose and reader shape what a description selects and withholds.|A visitor notices the locked museum door and the light still on inside.|A visitor lists every building in town without a focus.|The first makes selected details matter to a reader's curiosity.|A tired commuter notices the station's last pool of warmth.|A tired commuter inventories every object regardless of perspective.|Selection creates an experience rather than a catalogue.|Write a description for a reader arriving somewhere unfamiliar; select details that guide attention, then explain one omission.
A manageable moment leaves room to develop perception and change.|The story follows one attempt to return a lost glove.|The story compresses an entire childhood and three wars into one paragraph.|A small span permits developed action and consequence.|One minute beside a hospital door becomes the focus.|Every event in a family's history receives one sentence.|A chosen moment gives detail and pacing room to work.|Choose one minute in which a character hesitates; write only that minute, with a clear change of attention.
Sensory details should be selected for their contribution to the scene.|The kettle's click interrupts a conversation that has stopped.|The scene mechanically includes one example of every sense.|A meaningful sound relates to the moment rather than a checklist.|The sour smell makes a visitor reconsider the inviting fruit stall.|A list of unrelated smells is added to meet a quota.|The sensory change develops the visitor's response.|Describe a familiar place when one sensory detail changes its atmosphere; keep the response grounded in the scene.
Concrete details make a scene specific without excessive decoration.|A chipped enamel cup hangs from the gate.|A very amazing and wonderful object is somewhere nearby.|Material, damage and position supply useful precision.|A damp ticket sticks to the passenger's palm.|A good thing creates a strong feeling.|The small object connects touch with the situation.|Describe an object through three precise choices and one action; remove an adjective that contributes nothing.
Description can develop through an observer's movement.|The doorway frames the hall before a closer view reveals a torn programme.|The room's objects appear in an arbitrary order.|Movement provides a reason for the changing selection.|A walker follows the wall until a warm patch of brick interrupts the cold.|A stationary narrator claims every view at once without explanation.|The route organises the unfolding sensory experience.|Write a description whose focus changes because the observer moves through the setting.
A change of atmosphere needs a perceptible trigger and proportionate development.|The market's cheerful clatter stops when a shutter falls.|The atmosphere becomes terrifying because the narrator says so.|A specific interruption changes the established soundscape.|A friendly kitchen grows awkward after an unanswered question.|The writer adds suddenly before an unrelated event.|The contrast develops from the same setting and interaction.|Write a scene that shifts from ease to uncertainty through one concrete event; avoid announcing the emotion first.
A setting description can imply possibilities without racing through a plot.|An empty chair and a second cup invite questions about absence.|Ten dramatic events occur before the room is established.|Selected arrangement creates interest without requiring many events.|Footprints stop at a closed door in an otherwise quiet corridor.|A chase, a fire and a rescue are added to every paragraph.|The detail gives the reader something to interpret within the setting.|Describe a place just after somebody has left; suggest their presence without explaining a whole story.
Viewpoint controls which sensations and knowledge are available.|The child notices the underside of the table and the shoes beside it.|The child's first-person voice reports everyone's private thoughts.|The observer's position limits and shapes access.|A night guard hears a sound before identifying its source.|A limited narrator instantly knows a stranger's complete past.|Restricting knowledge permits a credible process of discovery.|Write from a chosen observer and audit every sentence for knowledge that observer could not have.
A descriptive paragraph should develop a focused relation among details.|The paragraph moves from light on the floor to the hand entering it.|Each sentence describes an unrelated item in a different place.|The details combine through focus and movement.|A paragraph follows the diminishing sound of a train.|A paragraph changes topic whenever a new adjective is found.|The organised sensory pattern gives the paragraph direction.|Write a paragraph built around one changing sound or shape; explain the relationship between its first and last detail.
An ending can leave a developed image without explaining its moral.|The unused cup cools beside the open door.|The narrator explains every character's entire future.|The final image returns to the scene's unresolved relation.|The last light moves off the empty chair.|The final line tells the reader exactly what emotion to feel.|The ending invites a response through a concrete development.|End a description by altering an earlier detail; avoid adding a sudden unrelated catastrophe.
A description plan organises focus rather than just collecting techniques.|I plan a wide view, one close detail and a changed final image.|I plan to include five devices regardless of the setting.|The plan gives the reader's attention a route.|I select three stages in the sound of a closing shop.|I list ten impressive words without a scene.|An organised sequence supports later choices of language.|Plan and write a description in three movements; after drafting, compare the actual focus with your plan.
Independent description needs a controlled scene, not a device demonstration.|A draft develops its selected moment through connected sensory detail.|A draft inserts a simile into each sentence regardless of meaning.|The choices should serve a coherent reading experience.|A fresh setting is shaped around a visible change.|A memorised paragraph ignores the new setting.|Transfer requires adapting selection to the actual task.|Complete the independent description task before consulting criteria; leave time to reread for coherence and accuracy.
Descriptive feedback should identify a passage-level effect and a concrete improvement.|A reader points to the lost spatial relation between two details.|A reader requests more adjectives in every sentence.|The feedback diagnoses how the description is experienced.|A reader identifies an abrupt change of viewpoint and explains its effect.|A reader awards a grade without reading the passage.|Useful feedback refers to actual language and organisation.|Ask a reader where they lose their sense of place; record their response and choose one revision goal.
A descriptive redraft should respond to a diagnosed weakness.|I reconnect a close detail to the observer's position.|I replace every word with a longer synonym.|The revision repairs a relation rather than inflating vocabulary.|I remove a repeated image so the final one develops more clearly.|I add an unrelated metaphor to prove I can.|The change supports the pattern already established.|Bring forward your description and revise a specific focus or viewpoint problem; preserve the original version.
Delayed description transfer tests flexible selection in a new situation.|I change my sensory pattern for the new setting.|I reuse the exact market paragraph for a silent bedroom.|Different conditions require different meaningful details.|A new observer notices a different part of the same place.|Every observer describes identical details in identical order.|Perspective shapes what is selected.|Write a new description without opening the earlier draft; compare one deliberate adaptation afterwards.
An action opening still needs enough orientation to be intelligible.|The rope slipped from Inez's hands as the boat moved from the jetty.|A rapid list of unidentified movements removes every spatial relation.|The action establishes a person, an object and a problem.|The key snapped in the station locker before the train was called.|The word bang is repeated without an identifiable event.|The problem is immediate but can still be pictured.|Write a narrative opening in action; establish who is acting, where they are and what has changed.
A setting opening can generate narrative pressure through a selected disturbance.|The empty platform contains one suitcase still turning on its side.|A page describes scenery without any relation to a possible event.|The small disturbance gives the setting a narrative question.|A lit window stands above a row of closed shops.|All background details receive equal space before the scene begins.|Selection makes a possible destination or obstacle noticeable.|Open a story through setting and one detail that creates a question; delay the full explanation.
A character's aim becomes dramatic when an obstacle demands a response.|She wants to return the parcel before the office closes, but the label is wet.|The character has no aim and every event is accidental.|The obstacle tests action towards a specific aim.|He needs the spare key but cannot wake the sleeping owner.|The story lists wishes without requiring any decision.|The tension comes from a constrained choice.|Give a character one immediate aim and a credible obstacle; write the decision rather than a biography.
Dialogue should change knowledge, pressure or relationship.|I kept it for you, she says, moving the unopened letter away.|Characters greet each other for a page without changing anything.|Speech and action can create a meaningful mismatch.|Which door? he asks, after she has already pointed twice.|Dialogue explains facts both speakers already know without a reason.|The question changes our sense of attention or uncertainty.|Write six lines of dialogue in which an object or decision becomes more significant; keep speaker changes clear.
Gesture can reveal a response without naming an emotion.|She folds the receipt until its printed total disappears.|She is sad, upset and unhappy, with no observable action.|The purposeful handling lets a reader infer a relation to the object.|He leaves his hand on the door after saying goodbye.|He is a completely good person because he smiles.|The gesture can complicate the spoken departure without deciding an entire character.|Write a character response through gesture; give enough context for an inference but do not explain every motive.
Narrative distance can move between an external action and closer perception.|From the far doorway she looks composed; close up, her fingers miss the clasp.|The narrator changes person and tense accidentally.|The change of access develops rather than contradicts the viewpoint.|A crowd is described before one participant's uneasy thought is given.|Every private thought appears without an established narrative perspective.|Distance and access require control.|Write a controlled move from an outside view to close perception; explain the new access it gives the reader.
Consequences should follow credibly from the situation and the character's choices.|The unread note explains why the visitor waits at the wrong entrance.|A random accident solves every problem with no preparation.|The consequence grows from a detail already established.|A hidden stain becomes visible when the sleeve is rolled up.|A stranger introduces a new solution unconnected with the scene.|Cause and consequence make the turn earned.|Write an action and its plausible consequence; plant the detail that makes the consequence intelligible.
A short narrative often benefits from a deliberately bounded time span.|The story covers the ten minutes before a meeting.|The story rushes across seventy years without developing a scene.|A limited span leaves room for chosen detail and change.|The narrator stays with one interrupted journey across a square.|Every sentence begins years later regardless of the central event.|A clear boundary helps sustain focus.|Write a short narrative taking place within a single interval; use summary only where it serves that scene.
A narrative turn changes understanding through prepared new information.|The parcel's return address connects it with an earlier unexplained call.|The ending introduces an unmentioned twin to explain everything.|The turn uses an existing detail rather than an unsupported rescue.|The lost key is found in the pocket the character refused to check.|A new magical power solves an otherwise realistic story without preparation.|A credible revelation tests a prior choice or assumption.|Plan a turn using a detail planted earlier; write both moments and explain their connection.
Pacing changes the amount of attention given to an event.|A year is summarised before the first meeting is shown closely.|Every event receives exactly one sentence regardless of significance.|Selective expansion gives a scene weight.|The walk is compressed until the hand reaches the unfamiliar lock.|The writer speeds up merely by adding exclamation marks.|The focus on one action can prolong anticipation.|Write the same event once in summary and once as a scene, then choose where each belongs.

A change of narrative distance should have a purpose and a clear return.|A broad view narrows into one person's perception during a decisive moment.|The narrator jumps between private minds without establishing that access.|The narrowing can make a small choice significant.|An observer's attention widens after leaving the crowded room.|The account changes viewpoint solely to explain missing information conveniently.|Distance should follow the selected experience.|Rewrite a passage at a different distance and explain what is gained and lost.
An unresolved opening can close a shaped scene without finishing a whole plot.|The visitor reaches the door and hears the voice they hoped to avoid.|The opening ends mid-sentence because time has run out.|A local turn can complete an opening while preserving a larger question.|The package is opened enough to reveal an unexpected name.|The narrator explains every later outcome in a rushed final line.|The partial revelation gives the opening a deliberate destination.|Write a story opening that reaches a meaningful turn but leaves the wider outcome open.
A complete short narrative needs a proportionate resolution or purposeful remaining uncertainty.|A character's changed action resolves the immediate obstacle.|The narrator announces it was all a dream to erase the developed problem.|The ending should emerge from the scene's choices.|A decision about the letter resolves the character's immediate aim but not every future relationship.|An unrelated explosion replaces the central decision.|A modest resolution can be complete without explaining a lifetime.|Write a complete short narrative centred on one decision and a consequence, then test whether the ending grows from the opening.
A narrative plan maps desire, obstacle, development and turn.|I choose a small aim, a blocked action and a consequential decision.|I collect dramatic incidents without a relationship.|A causal plan gives the draft a coherent route.|I select an image that can change meaning at the end.|I decide every paragraph must contain an unrelated flashback.|The plan prepares development rather than a device quota.|Plan two possible narrative routes for the same prompt and justify the more manageable one before drafting.
An independent opening adapts a fresh stimulus rather than reproducing a prepared plot.|The opening uses the new object to generate a specific obstacle.|A memorised chase is inserted regardless of the supplied scene.|Adapting makes the task's conditions matter.|A changed viewpoint creates a new question from the supplied location.|The writer copies a model's exact language as original work.|Independent composition makes deliberate choices rather than reproducing a model.|Write the independent story opening; keep the first submission unchanged before review.
A narrative conference should address the reader's actual experience of cause, viewpoint or pace.|The reader identifies where a decision becomes unclear and cites a line.|The reader says add a twist without identifying the existing narrative.|Specific feedback can guide a meaningful redraft.|The reader explains where a scene lingers without contributing to the turn.|The reader requests action in every sentence as a universal rule.|Pace should be judged against the intended effect.|Ask a reader to retell the aim and obstacle; compare that account with your intention and record the mismatch.
A narrative redraft should change the organisation or language that caused the problem.|I move the necessary clue before the revelation and adjust the transition.|I change the font and call the story revised.|The reader now has information needed to understand the turn.|I replace an unexplained viewpoint shift with a detail the observer can perceive.|I add a generic adjective to every line.|A revised access pattern improves coherence.|Bring forward your opening, select a conference point and show the revised passage beside the original.

# viewpoint-writing
Audience, purpose and form determine the appropriate relation with a reader.|A council letter explains a local problem and requests a specific change.|A letter uses a speech greeting and never identifies a request.|The form serves a real communicative purpose.|An article introduces an issue for readers who have not followed the debate.|An article assumes every reader knows all background details.|Audience awareness shapes the explanation supplied.|Write a short opening for the same issue as a letter and an article; explain the different choices.
A central viewpoint should be specific enough to guide an argument.|The school should trial a quiet study room at lunchtimes.|Everything about education is either perfect or terrible.|The bounded proposal permits reasons and evaluation.|The town should extend weekend library access on a trial basis.|Libraries are good, so every possible policy must be accepted.|A usable position identifies a decision rather than a vague allegiance.|State a qualified central position on a school or community issue; name the change you want and its limits.
An article opening should establish an issue and a reason to keep reading.|A familiar scene introduces the practical cost of an early library closure.|The article starts with an unrelated dictionary definition and never reaches the issue.|The opening connects the reader with the argument's stakes.|A short contrast frames who gains and who loses from a proposal.|A string of rhetorical questions substitutes for a claim.|The contrast creates a direction that later paragraphs can develop.|Write two article openings for the same position and justify which gives your reader the clearer route.
A formal letter balances conventions with a purposeful, appropriate tone.|A named recipient is addressed respectfully before the specific request.|The letter threatens the reader instead of explaining the request.|A clear relationship and request make the communication usable.|A complaint identifies the problem and proposes a realistic remedy.|The closing introduces a different issue not discussed earlier.|The letter's parts need a coherent purpose.|Write a formal letter requesting a change; check salutation, purpose, tone and closing without treating layout as the whole task.
A public speech must orient listeners who cannot reread each sentence.|Clear signposts connect a central proposal with a concrete example.|Long unexplained pronouns force listeners to reconstruct the argument.|Auditory clarity depends on accessible connections.|A key phrase returns at a meaningful point after a counterargument.|A phrase is repeated after every sentence regardless of development.|Repetition can help memory when it carries changing significance.|Draft a speech opening and deliver it aloud; note where a listener needs an extra signpost.
A discursive essay weighs a question through organised reasoning.|The essay explains competing priorities before reaching a qualified conclusion.|Each paragraph states a preference without examining reasons.|Discursive development considers the issue rather than staging unrelated opinions.|A concession is weighed against the main proposal's practical benefit.|A concession appears only as a token sentence and is never answered.|Competing reasons need a relation and a judgment.|Write a short discursive response to a policy question; make the conclusion follow the reasons actually developed.
Evidence must be relevant, accurately represented and honestly sourced.|The writer labels a hypothetical example as hypothetical.|The writer invents a survey and presents it as verified research.|Honest provenance matters more than an impressive-looking number.|A supplied fact is used only for the claim it can support.|A small local example is treated as proof of every national outcome.|The scope of the evidence limits the scope of the claim.|Use a supplied fact or a clearly labelled hypothetical example; explain what it supports and what it cannot prove.
Explanation connects a claim with a reason and consequence.|A quiet room could help students who lack a calm place elsewhere because it reduces one practical barrier.|A quiet room is good because it is good.|The causal link makes the reason assessable.|A proposal's cost is explained in relation to its intended benefit.|A writer repeats the proposal with stronger adjectives.|An argument develops when it explains rather than merely intensifies.|Develop one claim through a causal explanation and a relevant example; test an alternative explanation.
A paragraph should advance a distinct part of the argument.|A paragraph develops access before the next considers staffing.|Every paragraph repeats the same opening claim.|Distinct reasons permit cumulative development.|An example is interpreted before the paragraph moves to its implication.|Three examples are listed without any explanation.|Evidence needs a role within the reasoning.|Write one complete argument paragraph and explain what new work it does in the whole response.

Coherence requires clear relations between claims, not a list of connectives.|Because access is the aim, the proposed hours match the times students need it.|Furthermore is placed between two unrelated claims.|The connection is logical as well as linguistic.|A paragraph refers back to the exact concern raised in the previous one.|This is repeated when its reference is unclear.|Clear reference helps a reader follow the argument.|Link three reasons into a coherent sequence and remove any connective that hides a missing relation.
Persuasive detail should make the issue concrete without falsifying evidence.|A clearly hypothetical student misses the bus after the library closes.|A fictional pupil is described as a real survey participant.|The example can illustrate a barrier when its status is honest.|A description of a locked entrance supports a local access argument.|A dramatic detail is invented and called an eyewitness fact.|Vividness does not excuse misrepresentation.|Write a persuasive example labelled as real, supplied or hypothetical; explain why that detail is relevant.
A rhetorical question should sharpen an issue that the argument develops.|If the room is empty at lunch, why not test a supervised study session? leads into practical conditions.|Why is everything bad? replaces any specific proposal.|The question introduces a bounded decision rather than vague outrage.|Who can use the service when the doors close before they arrive? is followed by an access argument.|Ten questions appear without any attempt to reason through them.|The device needs a role in the developing case.|Write one purposeful rhetorical question and the reasoning that follows it; remove a question that merely repeats the claim.
